**CONTROLLING INTELLIGENCE**

*Export Controls and America\'s Lever of Power in the AI Era*

A Book Proposal

by Will Hunt

OVERVIEW

In the fall of 2024, I stepped into Secretary Gina Raimondo\'s
conference room at the Commerce Department to propose a solution to a
problem that had quietly shaken the foundations of our export control
strategy.

A research firm had taken apart Huawei\'s most advanced AI
chip---physically disassembled it, examined the silicon under a
microscope---to determine where it came from. The answer was TSMC, the
world\'s leading chipmaker. It had been illegal for TSMC to manufacture
chips for Huawei since 2020.

Millions of chips had flowed to China\'s leading technology firm through
a shell company, while we congratulated ourselves on the effectiveness
of our controls. I was there to explain why the problem was harder than
it looked---and what we could do about it.

The episode captured something important about the challenge the United
States faces in semiconductor policy. We have real leverage: a single
Dutch company, ASML, produces every machine capable of printing the most
advanced chips, and the knowledge required to build these systems cannot
be transferred or reverse-engineered on any reasonable timeline.
American companies dominate the chemicals, gases, and design software
without which advanced semiconductors cannot exist. This is a genuine
chokepoint, controlled by the United States and a handful of allies, and
China cannot replicate it.

But leverage is not the same as success. We are trying to cut off a
nation-state that has spent decades mastering technology transfer, that
has come to dominate industry after industry through aggressive
industrial policy, and that has made semiconductors a core national
priority. The policy challenge is whether we can adapt our controls
faster than China adapts to them---and so far, that question remains
open.

The stakes are significant. Advanced chips underpin modern military
systems, from precision weapons to autonomous drones. They are embedded
in most of the economy, from cars to medical devices. And increasingly,
they are the hardware on which artificial intelligence runs---the
physical infrastructure that determines which nations can train the most
capable systems and deploy them at scale. Some researchers believe that
AI progress may accelerate dramatically in the coming years, as AI
systems become capable of improving AI systems. I am not certain this
will happen, but policymakers should take the possibility seriously. If
it does, control over semiconductor production will matter even more
than it does today.

We have real strengths in this competition, but also real weaknesses.
The agency responsible for semiconductor export controls, the Bureau of
Industry and Security, has nine overseas enforcement officers; one or
two focus on China at any given time. When they attempt to verify
end-use, Chinese officials provide escorts, restrict access, and limit
what questions can be asked. Our controls have been more
forward-thinking than most people realize, but enforcement has not kept
pace with ambition.

Our vulnerabilities extend beyond enforcement. The most advanced chips
in the world are manufactured in Taiwan, an island China claims as its
own, within range of Chinese missiles. The CHIPS Act has begun to
address this concentration, but progress has been slow and expensive,
and the results remain uncertain.

This book argues that we can do better---that with clearer thinking and
more aggressive action, we can widen the gap between American and
Chinese AI capabilities, deploy AI throughout our economy and military
faster than our adversary, and buy ourselves time to manage what may be
a turbulent technological transition.

**Part I, \"The Lever,\"** establishes what we have and why it matters.
The chokepoint is real, but it is not intuitive---most readers, even
sophisticated ones, do not understand why a nation-state with China\'s
resources cannot simply build its own lithography machines. I explain
how the chokepoint came to exist, why it has proven durable, and what it
means for the distribution of power in the twenty-first century. I then
build the case for urgency: chips as military advantage, chips as
economic infrastructure, and chips as the hardware on which AI runs. The
section concludes with Taiwan---why the island\'s centrality to the
supply chain is both a vulnerability and a source of leverage.

**Part II, \"The Record,\"** examines what the United States has
actually done with this leverage over the past five years.

The effort had two dimensions: building capacity at home through the
CHIPS Act, and denying it to China through export controls. Both were
bolder than most observers recognize, and both achieved real results.
But both also ran into the same fundamental problem: the difficulty of
regulating fast-moving technology against an adversary that adapts
quickly.

The CHIPS Act mobilized tens of billions of dollars to bring
semiconductor manufacturing back to American soil. This section examines
what worked, what didn\'t, and why.

Export controls told a parallel story. The rules imposed in 2022 were
the most aggressive use of this tool in a generation. Within months,
companies had designed around them. We tightened the rules; new
workarounds emerged. By the end, we had built a regulatory architecture
unlike anything that existed before---genuinely innovative in places,
and still not adequate to the task.

**Part III, \"The Path Forward,\"** asks what it would mean to use this
lever effectively.

The answer requires holding two ideas simultaneously. The first is that
we need to be more aggressive---gaps remain in our controls, some
because we moved too slowly, others because we hesitated to follow the
logic of our own policies. Closing them will be controversial.

The second is that denial alone is not a strategy. The goal is not to
hurt China but to preserve American advantage long enough to navigate
what may be a disruptive technological transition. If we succeed, we
will have bought ourselves not just a lead but leverage---the ability to
engage from strength rather than desperation. The book concludes by
asking what that engagement might look like, and what it would take to
manage a competition that neither side fully controls.

The book ends with a priority list for the next administration. The
decisions made in the coming years will shape whether the United States
maintains its advantage in semiconductor technology or watches it erode.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Will Hunt is Professional Staff Member for technology on the House
Select Committee on the Strategic Competition Between the United States
and the Chinese Communist Party, majority staff, a position he has held
since September 2025.

From 2023 to 2025, he served as a Senior Advisor at the Bureau of
Industry and Security, where he worked on semiconductor export controls
across the final year of the Biden administration and the opening months
of the second Trump administration. He was involved in the response to
the SophGo diversion and the development of the foundry due diligence
rule.

From 2022 to 2023, he was a Senior Policy Advisor in the Commerce
Department\'s CHIPS Program Office, where he worked on the design and
implementation of fab manufacturing incentives.

From 2019 to 2022, he led the semiconductor supply chain portfolio at
Georgetown University\'s Center for Security and Emerging Technology
(CSET), where he authored influential research on CHIPS Act
implementation, workforce challenges, and the strategic importance of
different chip types. His work was cited in the Wall Street Journal, the
Financial Times, and other major outlets, and helped shape the policy
debates that led to the CHIPS and Science Act.

Hunt has testified before the Strategic Technologies and Advanced
Research (STAR) Subcommittee of the House Permanent Select Committee on
Intelligence. He has authored op-eds in the Wall Street Journal and has
been quoted in the New York Times, Washington Post, Wired, and MIT
Technology Review, among other outlets.

He attended Deep Springs College and holds a BA from Yale University and
an MA from the University of California, Berkeley, where he is
completing a PhD in Political Science.

TARGET AUDIENCE

The primary audience is the general readership interested in US-China
competition and technology---the readers who made *Chip War* a
bestseller. *Chip War* was written by a historian and covers seventy
years of semiconductor history; it was published in 2022, before most of
the export control actions described in this book had taken place, and
before the release of ChatGPT made AI a mainstream concern. This book is
written by a policymaker, covers the past five years in depth, and puts
AI and AI-specialized chips at the center of the story. Readers who
finished *Chip War* wanting to know what happens next---and what the
United States should do about it---are the core audience.

A secondary audience is the policy community: administration officials,
congressional staff, think tank researchers, and journalists covering
technology and national security. The book could be assigned alongside
*Chip War* in a graduate seminar---or read on its own by anyone who
wants to understand in more depth than *Chip War* provided exactly what
is happening right now in semiconductor policy. I want it to have the
specificity that will make it useful in policy circles: cited in
debates, read in transition briefings, referenced in congressional
hearings.

A third audience is the technology industry: executives, investors, and
government affairs professionals who need to understand where
semiconductor policy is heading. Export controls and industrial policy
directly affect their businesses, and the book offers an insider
perspective on how these policies are made and where they are likely to
go.

COMPARABLE TITLES

**Chip War: The Fight for the World\'s Most Critical Technology** by
Chris Miller (Scribner, 2022). The most direct comparison and the book
this one follows. *Chip War* won the Financial Times Business Book of
the Year, became a *New York Times* bestseller, and shaped how a broad
audience thinks about semiconductors. It is a history, ending roughly
where this book begins. Readers who want to understand what has happened
since 2022---and what should happen next---are the natural audience for
*Controlling Intelligence*.

**The Long Game: China\'s Grand Strategy to Displace American Order** by
Rush Doshi (Oxford University Press, 2021). A deeply researched account
of Chinese strategy that was widely read in the Biden administration;
Doshi joined the National Security Council after publication. It
demonstrates that a policy book with real depth can influence both
public debate and government action. *Controlling Intelligence* aims for
similar influence on semiconductor and AI policy.

**World on the Brink: How America Can Beat China in the Race for the
Twenty-First Century** by Dmitri Alperovitch (PublicAffairs, 2024). A
national security practitioner\'s account of US-China competition,
focused on Taiwan. Alperovitch\'s book shows the appetite for
forward-looking policy arguments from authors with operational
experience, not just academic credentials.

**Chokepoints: American Power in the Age of Economic Warfare** by Edward
Fishman (Portfolio, 2025). An insider account of economic statecraft by
a former State Department sanctions official. The closest analogue in
terms of author profile---a former government official writing about the
tools of economic power---though Fishman\'s focus is broader and less
technical than mine.

CHAPTER OUTLINE

Part I: The Lever

**Chapter 1: The Stakes**

This chapter makes the case for why AI---and the chips that power
it---has become the central front in US-China competition.

The first argument is military and intelligence. AI is transforming how
wars are fought and how intelligence is gathered, from autonomous drones
to signals processing to battlefield decision-making. The chapter
examines how the PLA is integrating AI into its doctrine and
capabilities, and what that means for American military advantage. It
also addresses how AI enables surveillance and repression---the tools
the CCP uses domestically are increasingly exported to other
authoritarian regimes, with implications for human rights worldwide.

The second argument is economic. US-China competition may ultimately be
decided by which country can sustain higher growth over the coming
decades, and growth depends increasingly on productivity gains from AI.
China has four times our population, but if the United States can
diffuse AI more effectively through its economy---enabled by superior
access to the chips that make AI systems run---we may be able to outgrow
them despite the demographic gap. The chapter examines what AI-driven
growth might look like and why chip access is the binding constraint.

The third argument is the intelligence explosion. Some researchers
believe that AI progress could accelerate dramatically if AI systems
become capable of improving AI systems. I do not claim certainty about
this, but national security policymakers must take the possibility
seriously---and increasingly, they already do. If it happens, the
country that controls the hardware will have an advantage that compounds
over time. The chapter explains the argument, assesses its plausibility,
and draws out the implications for semiconductor policy.

**Chapter 2: The Supply Chain**

This chapter teaches readers how advanced chips are actually made, from
first principles. It is not a technical manual, but it gives readers the
foundation they need to understand why the supply chain has chokepoints
and where they are.

The chapter begins with the basics: what a transistor is, how chips are
designed, and how those designs become physical objects. It explains the
role of lithography---the process of printing circuits onto
silicon---and why extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography is so difficult
that only one company in the world can do it. It covers the other key
elements of advanced chip production: high-bandwidth memory, advanced
packaging, and the chemicals and gases that enable manufacturing.

The chapter then turns to geography. It explains why Taiwan has become
the center of advanced chipmaking, the role of TSMC, and the risks posed
by this concentration. It examines the scenarios that concern
policymakers---invasion, blockade, coercion---and the prediction
markets\' assessment of their likelihood. By the end, readers understand
both the technical foundations and the strategic vulnerability.

Part II: The Record

**Chapter 3: Building at Home**

This chapter examines the American effort to build semiconductor and AI
infrastructure domestically, centered on the CHIPS Act but extending to
the broader datacenter buildout now underway.

On the CHIPS Act, I offer an honest assessment based on my time in the
program office. The investment tax credit proved more powerful than
anticipated. The decision to derisk TSMC\'s Arizona expansion was sound.
But the program also accumulated mandates---childcare, workforce
requirements---that slowed implementation without advancing its core
mission. The largest grant went to Intel, a company whose problems trace
back a decade. Billions went to Samsung on a strategic logic that
remains unclear to me.

The chapter also examines the datacenter buildout: the scale of current
investment, the energy requirements, and the policy debates around
permitting and electricity prices. I address common
misconceptions---permitting reform matters but is not the primary
constraint; AI is not, for the most part, driving up electricity prices
for consumers. The chapter explains what is actually happening and what
it means for American capacity in AI.

**Chapter 4: Export Controls**

This chapter tells the story of American semiconductor export controls
targeting China, from the pressure on the Dutch to restrict EUV
equipment through the 2022 rules and their subsequent evolution.

The October 2022 rules were the most aggressive use of export controls
in a generation. I explain their logic: not just restricting chips but
restricting the tools to make chips, using the foreign direct product
rule to extend American jurisdiction worldwide. I also explain their
limitations---why an interconnect-speed carve-out allowed Nvidia to
design compliant chips that still served Chinese customers, and why it
took time to close that gap.

The chapter covers the SophGo episode in detail: how millions of chips
flowed to Huawei through a shell company, how the government discovered
it, and what we built in response. The foundry due diligence
rule---which presumes a chip is controlled until proven
otherwise---represents a genuine regulatory innovation. But I also
explain its limits. The current threshold is based on transistor count,
and Huawei will simply design smaller chips. The cycle of rule,
workaround, new rule continues.

The chapter also addresses enforcement: nine overseas officers, one or
two focused on China, MOFCOM escorts limiting what we can see. Our
ambitions have outpaced our capacity to verify compliance.

**Chapter 5: Allies and Third Countries**

Export controls only work if allies cooperate. This chapter examines how
the United States has managed---and sometimes mismanaged---relationships
with the other countries that matter for semiconductor policy.

The chapter begins with the Netherlands and Japan, the two countries
whose cooperation is essential because of their roles in chipmaking
equipment. It covers the negotiations that led to aligned controls, the
tensions that arose, and the large flow of DUV tools to China that
occurred even after agreements were reached.

It then turns to the rest of the world: the diffusion rule\'s attempt to
create tiers of access, the Middle East deals with UAE and Saudi Arabia,
and the challenge of preventing transshipment through third countries.
The chapter assesses what has worked in allied coordination and what has
not, and what that implies for future policy.

Part III: The Path Forward

**Chapter 6: Proposals**

This chapter presents a package of proposals for American semiconductor
policy, some incremental and some likely to be controversial.

On export controls, I argue for controlling all chokepoint equipment to
China as a whole, ending the current approach of designating individual
facilities. I argue for extending controls to edge computing chips---the
processors that will power autonomous systems, from military drones to
humanoid robots. And I argue for placing export controls on Taiwan,
requiring that future chipmaking capacity be built elsewhere. This last
proposal will strike some readers as extreme, but the
alternative---continued concentration of the world\'s most important
supply chain in the most dangerous flashpoint---is untenable.

On engagement, I argue that the United States should use the leverage
that export controls provide to open discussions with China about
managing the transition ahead. This is not a call for naïve cooperation.
It is a recognition that a race with no off-ramps serves no one\'s
interests, and that we are more likely to achieve a stable outcome if we
negotiate from strength.

The chapter also covers proposals related to allies: what conditions we
should set for access to advanced technology, how to structure
investment requirements for Middle Eastern partners, and what a more
durable framework for allied coordination might look like.

**Chapter 7: Conclusion**

The final chapter steps back from the specific policy debates to
consider what is at stake.

It reviews the argument of the book: that the United States holds real
leverage over the semiconductor supply chain, that we have used it more
aggressively than most observers realize, and that the outcome of this
competition remains genuinely uncertain. It considers what success would
look like---not the destruction of Chinese technological capacity, but
the preservation of American advantage long enough to navigate a
potentially turbulent transition.

The chapter ends with a priority list: the most important actions, in
order, for whoever is in a position to act on them.
